Listen to Earth

NASA Earth Information Center animations, read pixel by pixel in your browser and played as piano: sweep a frame west to east and hear where the ice, fires or pollution are; touch the sea-ice map and hear ice or open water; hear 48 Septembers of Arctic sea ice thin a Debussy piece.

How the sea-ice data becomes sound

DataSound
September Arctic sea ice extent (million km²), one value per year, fixed scale 3–8How much of the piano plays: ≥5% top melody line, ≥30% + bass, above that the inner voices return one by one
The same numberA sine tone on a fixed D-dorian scale (D4–D6): higher = more ice
NarrationLowers the music volume only; never changes which notes play

Which notes exist, their pitch and rhythm are Debussy's. The data decides which of them are played. We do not claim that every note comes from data.

What the data can and cannot say

One September value per year, so this is a long-term comparison, not a seasonal cycle. Largest in this record: 1980 (7.67 million km², 20% above the 1981–2010 mean of 6.41). Smallest: 2012 (3.57, 44% below). It shows a decline since 1979 with large year-to-year swings; it does not explain causes and a single year can rebound.

How we chose the sound

We built seven very different ways of turning the animations into sound and let measurements, not taste, pick the finalists.

  1. Same input for all: 12 NASA EIC/SVS animations, 12 s each, read the same way (8 west→east strips + whole-frame share, legends masked).
  2. Machine listener: from the audio alone it checks whether different data sounds different in proportion (representational similarity, after Tan, Maul & Mennie, PLoS ONE 2013), whether you can hear how much and where; a learned aesthetics model (Meta Audiobox-Aesthetics) rates enjoyment and production quality; loudness and peak limits are enforced. Score = √(information × quality), 0–1.
  3. Unseen animations: every finalist was re-run, unchanged, on 12 other animations. Two methods dropped sharply and were removed (overfitted).
  4. Human ears decide: the four most different methods that held up went into a blind listening test (26 questions). Rhythm won: preferred in all three of its pairings, both "which has more" questions right, rated 4 of 5 for pleasantness. In round 2 the rhythm version with chords and a hook won again (preferred, both "which has more" right, 4 of 5); only the plain-rhythm version let the listener hear left/right. Round 3 had 100 questions: "which has more" was answered correctly 20 of 24 times (83%; guessing gives 50%), the chords-and-hook version tied with two versions made to sharpen position and beat plain rhythm 6 to 0, and left/right stayed weak (11 of 24), so position is also stated in words for screen-reader users. Each finalist exists twice: a curated soundtrack pre-rendered for all 25 animations at full length, and a live engine that reads the playing video in real time (also for your own videos). Live engines score a little lower because they cannot look ahead in time. These are proxies: they show the data is in the sound and how a model rates it, not how easily people hear it, which is why the human test decides.

Evidence

Listening test: not yet run. The test is ready: listening-test.html (narration off, picture hidden, pairs of the same phrase, answer "which has more ice"). We will report: how many listeners, whether they could tell more ice from less ice with narration off, picture hidden and numbers hidden, and where they struggled. No claim about blind or low-vision users is made until blind or low-vision listeners have tried it.

Accessibility built in: keyboard operation, screen-reader text, a "read this year's data" button, a "why does it sound like this" button, vibration cues on Android, English and Chinese text.
