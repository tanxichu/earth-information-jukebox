Listen to Earth

Pick a NASA Earth Information Center animation and hear it: the picture is read pixel by pixel and played on a piano. Then touch a sea-ice map, and hear 48 Septembers of Arctic sea ice. Best with headphones; turn off your phone's silent switch.

1. NASA EIC jukebox: pick an animation, hear it

Purpose: test whether people can hear a real NASA Earth Information Center (SVS) animation change. Technique: the animation is read pixel by pixel in the browser. Sweep mode scans each frame west→east in 8 strips every 2 seconds: each strip becomes one note (more of the feature = higher, louder; none = silence), so you hear where things are. Whole frame mode uses the whole frame's share of the feature to decide how many notes of a public-domain Debussy piece play. The feature is hand-picked for each animation (ice → white, fires and temperature → warm colours, vegetation and rain → green, smoke and carbon monoxide → deep red; logos, dates and colour legends are masked out); for other animations and your own videos it is the feature that changes most, and you can always choose it yourself.

2. Arctic sea ice: the guided listen (about 1 minute)

Touch the map, or focus it and use the arrow keys: a high piano note means ice, a low note means open water, silence means land. Press S to scan the current row left to right.

Choose the data

The guided listen above always uses Arctic sea ice. Sections 2 and 3 use the dataset chosen here. Same rule for all: the more of the measured thing, the more of the piano plays.

3. Same phrase, two years

Plays the same 8 seconds of music twice. Only the data changes, so every difference you hear comes from the ice.

3b. Same year, two measurements

Pick one year and two kinds of data. The same 8 seconds of music play for each, thinned by its own number. Note that for temperature and CO₂, a bigger number means more piano.

4. Explore the years

Keyboard: ← → change year, A / B play the two years, Space starts or stops the guided listen.

Settings

The piece is provisional and can be swapped; the data rules stay the same.

How it works, and what it can and cannot say

The rule

September Arctic sea ice extent (million km²) sets how much of the piano plays. The scale is fixed from 3 to 8 and never auto-scaled. From 5% of the scale the top melody line plays, from 30% the bass is added, above that the inner voices return one by one. A separate sine tone plays the same number as a pitch on a fixed D-dorian scale, D4 to D6: higher means more ice. With 'equal loudness' on (default) the music level is compensated so you hear fewer notes, not a quieter piano; turn it off to hear the raw level. Narration lowers the music volume only; it does not change which notes play, so narration is never mistaken for data.

What the data can say

One value per year, always September, so this is a long-term comparison, not a seasonal cycle. It shows a decline since 1979 with large year-to-year variation (lowest in this record: 2012, 3.57 million km², 44% below the 1981–2010 mean of 6.41). It does not say why, and a single year can bounce back.

What is data and what is Debussy

The notes, pitches and rhythm belong to Debussy. The data decides which of those notes are played, and a separate sine tone is generated from the data. We do not claim that every note comes from NASA data.
